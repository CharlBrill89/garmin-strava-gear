The drags

Both climbs
These are not hills Climb 1 is 5 km at 0.8%; climb 2 is 4 km. There is no pitch steep enough to make HR lag, so pace by HR and let the pace do what it does. Losing 12–15 s/km on a drag this long is correct, not a failure.
The spike you feel is not the one that counts On a 2.5–3% pitch your HR jumps 10–17 bpm within a few hundred metres. These drags are 0.8%, so expect nearer +3 to +5. Across 111 of your kilometre laps the relationship is only +1.8 bpm per 1% of gradient, because you already slow by about 5 s/km per 1% without thinking about it. Brushing 170 as you crest something is nothing; sitting at 170 for three minutes is the problem. Judge it on the rolling average.
Climb 1 is the rehearsal Km 6–10 tells you exactly how km 17–21 will feel, seven kilometres before it counts. Read it honestly at the 10 km gate.
Km 14 is the one free kilometre A 26 m drop in a single kilometre. Let it run — it should be your fastest split of the day and it costs almost nothing.
Short stride on both drops Km 3 and km 14 are where your quads get the bill. Reaching out downhill multiplies the braking force they absorb, and that is what is burning at the end of your runs. Quick turnover, short stride, stay relaxed — let gravity do it rather than catching yourself. You want those legs intact for the climb at 17.
Km 18 is the crisis The steepest kilometre of the race, +17 m, with 18 km in your legs. Your slowest 2024 split landed exactly here. Expect it to hurt, know it is one kilometre, and know that in 2024 you still came home at 5:11.

If HR looks wrong

Still post-viral
Two weeks out from a three-week illness, HR can misreport either way — commonly 5–10 bpm high at a given pace, occasionally blunted and low while you feel awful.
  • High HR at easy effort → trust the HR, back off.
  • Low HR but laboured breathing → trust the breathing, back off.
Asymmetric on purpose: whichever signal says slow down wins.
